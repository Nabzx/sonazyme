# 4. What a biofilm is

Bacteria floating alone in water are the exception. Given a surface, most
bacteria stick to it, multiply, and build a layer around themselves. That
layer, cells plus the material they secrete, is a **biofilm**. Dental plaque
is one. The slippery film on a river stone is one. The layer on the inside of
a cooling pipe is one.

## How one forms

1. **Attachment.** A few cells drift onto a surface and hold on loosely. In a
   pipe this happens most readily where flow is slow or the surface is rough.
2. **Sticking.** The cells switch on different genes and start secreting
   material that glues them down. Loose attachment becomes permanent.
3. **Growth.** The cells divide. The secreted material builds up around them
   into a matrix with the cells embedded in it.
4. **Maturity.** The film develops structure, with channels through it that
   carry water and nutrients inwards.
5. **Dispersal.** Pieces break off and travel downstream to start again
   elsewhere. This is why a problem in one part of a loop does not stay there.

## Why it is hard to get rid of

A biofilm is not a pile of loose bacteria. It is defended, in several ways at
once:

- The matrix is a physical barrier. A biocide has to soak through it, and gets
  used up reacting with the matrix on the way in, so cells deep inside see a
  much weaker dose than the one you added.
- Cells deep in the film grow slowly because nutrients run out. Many biocides
  work best on actively growing cells, so the slow ones are hit less hard.
- A small fraction of cells are in a dormant state that tolerates treatment
  almost regardless of dose. These survive, and they are enough to rebuild.
- Crucially, **killing the cells does not remove the matrix**. The structure
  stays stuck to the wall, and it is an ideal surface for the next cells to
  attach to. This is why a treated loop often fouls again quickly.

That last point is the whole argument for Sonazyme. Biocide attacks the
cells. We attack what holds the thing on the wall.

## Why it matters in a cooling loop

- **Heat transfer.** A biofilm is mostly water held in a gel, and it conducts
  heat poorly. A thin layer on a heat exchanger surface acts as insulation on
  exactly the surface whose job is to move heat.
- **Flow.** The layer narrows the channel and roughens the wall, so pumps work
  harder for the same flow.
- **Corrosion.** Some bacteria change the chemistry at the metal surface
  underneath them, which can drive localised corrosion. Sulfate-reducing
  bacteria are the usual suspects, and they live happily in the low-oxygen
  layer at the bottom of a film.
