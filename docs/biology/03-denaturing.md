# 3. Denaturing

Heat is movement. The hotter something is, the more its molecules vibrate. A
protein in warm water is a chain being shaken.

Up to a point, heat helps an enzyme: molecules meet more often, so the
reaction goes faster. Past a point, the shaking starts breaking the weak
interactions that hold the fold together, the hydrogen bonds and salt bridges
from the last page. Enough of them go and the fold collapses. That is
**denaturing**.

A denatured enzyme is not damaged chemically. The chain is intact, every amino
acid still there in the same order. The shape is gone, so the active site is
gone, so the activity is gone. In most cases it does not come back, because
unfolded chains stick to each other and clump instead of refolding.

## The optimum temperature curve

Plot an enzyme's rate against temperature and you get a lopsided hill. Rate
climbs steadily, peaks at the **optimum temperature**, then falls off a cliff.
The climb is faster molecular movement. The cliff is denaturing. The fall is
much steeper than the rise, which is why running an enzyme a few degrees above
its optimum is not a small compromise.

Each enzyme has its own optimum, set by what its organism evolved in. Human
enzymes peak near 37 °C. Enzymes from bacteria living in hot springs can work
well above 80 °C, and those are a useful place to look for starting material.

## pH does the same thing differently

Several amino acid side groups carry a charge, and whether they carry it
depends on the acidity of the water around them. Change the pH and you change
which groups are charged, which breaks the salt bridges holding the fold and
can also stop the active site from gripping its substrate.

So an enzyme has an optimum pH as well, and the same cliff on either side of
it. A cooling loop is deliberately run slightly alkaline to protect the metal,
which is away from where many useful enzymes are happiest.

## Two numbers you will see

- **Tm**, the melting temperature: the temperature at which half of the
  protein has unfolded. A higher Tm means a tougher protein.
- **ΔΔG** (said "delta delta G"): how much a change to the sequence alters the
  stability of the fold, compared with the original. The sign convention
  varies between tools, so Sonazyme always records which tool produced the
  number alongside it.

## Why this is the whole problem

An enzyme that cuts biofilm matrix beautifully at 30 °C and denatures at 45 °C
is of no use in a loop running hotter than that. Buying one off the shelf does
not fix it, because the shelf is stocked for laundry and food processing.

Two possible ways out. Make the fold tougher, which is what this project does,
or find a tough starting enzyme that already does the job, which is rarer than
it sounds. Both are covered on
[page 8](08-what-makes-a-protein-stable.md).
