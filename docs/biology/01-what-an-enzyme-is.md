# 1. What an enzyme is

An enzyme is a biological catalyst. A catalyst speeds up a chemical reaction
without being used up, so one enzyme molecule does the same job over and over,
thousands of times a second in some cases.

Enzymes are proteins. A protein is a long chain of smaller units called amino
acids, folded into a specific three-dimensional shape.

## The active site

Somewhere on that folded shape there is a pocket called the **active site**.
The molecule the enzyme works on is called the **substrate**. The substrate
fits into the active site like a key into a lock, the reaction happens there,
and the products leave.

This is why enzymes are **specific**: a given enzyme has a pocket shaped for a
given substrate and will not work on something else. An enzyme that cuts up
starch does nothing to protein.

A slightly better picture than lock and key is **induced fit**: the active
site is not rigid, and it shifts a little to close around the substrate when
it arrives. Keep that in mind later, because it is the reason making an enzyme
stiffer can also make it slower.

## Why a catalyst helps

Every reaction needs a push to get started, called the activation energy.
Without an enzyme the push usually comes from heat, which is why boiling
things makes reactions go faster. An enzyme provides a different route with a
smaller push needed, so the reaction runs quickly at ordinary temperatures.

Breaking down a biofilm is a reaction like any other. It happens on its own,
extremely slowly. The right enzyme makes it happen fast enough to matter.

## Naming

Most enzyme names end in **-ase**, and the first part says what they act on.

- A **protease** cuts protein.
- A **DNase** cuts DNA.
- A **glycoside hydrolase** cuts the links between sugars in a sugar chain.
- An **amylase** cuts starch, which is a particular sugar chain.

When you see an enzyme name in this repo, the ending tells you which part of
the biofilm it is aimed at.
